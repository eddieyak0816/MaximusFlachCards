Variables

Variables are things that can vary, or change. Some variables reflect a change over time. The temperature at noon is a variable because it varies from day to day, depending on the weather and time of year. If you collect this data for many years, you can predict the temperature at noon for a particular date. You would base your prediction on how warm and cold it has been on that same date in the past.

When a measurement changes from subject to subject, it is also a variable. Height is a variable because it changes from person to person.



There are two types of variables:

Independent variables are the ones you start within an experiment. These are independent of what happens in the experiment. Scientists may apply or change variables to see what effect they have.

Dependent variables are what you want to learn about by performing your experiment. They are changes that depend on the independent variables.

Random samples

Illustration of a simple random sample (Courtesy: www.geeksforgeeks.org)

Random samples help scientists collect accurate data. A random sample means that each object in the sample has an equal chance of being chosen from the overall set of objects being studied. For example, a farmer wants to know how many insects are in his apple orchards. He would not have a good sample if he only selected trees near the road. A better option is to randomly select the trees from all over the orchard.

3 Kinds of Averages

One skill you will need for your test is being able to calculate an average.

An average is a value that summarizes or represents a group of numbers. It can be calculated in different ways, depending on what you want to know about the set. Here are the most common types of averages:

Mean: The most commonly used average, which gives you a general idea of the overall group. You find the mean by adding all the numbers in the set together and dividing by the number of items.

Median: The middle value in a set when the numbers are arranged in order. If the set has an even number of items, the median is the average of the two middle numbers.

Mode: The most frequent number in the set—the number that appears most often.

Another important concept is range, which measures the spread of the numbers. It is the difference between the largest and smallest values in a set. For example, in the set (6, 8, 8, 8, 10, 11, 12), the range is calculated by subtracting the smallest number (6) from the largest number (12), resulting in a range of 6.

How might this show up in a test?

You will likely need to calculate the mean, but questions could also involve finding the median or mode, or determining the range. These problems might be presented as word problems, through tables of numbers, or as a combination of both.

It's important to know how to calculate each of these to be well-prepared for your test.

Now let’s look at the structure of cells. All eukaryotic cells are made of the same basic parts. Here are some of them:

The nucleus is the cell’s headquarters. It controls what the cell does and how it is made.

Chromosomes hold the information that tells the cell what it is and how to behave.

Cytoplasm surrounds the nucleus. It holds various smaller parts.

Mitochondria (plural of mitochondria) process oxygen and food to make energy, in the form of ATP, for the cell.

The cell membrane is like skin. It controls what goes in or out of the cell.

There are other parts to cells as well as those labeled above. Any parts that have a function are called organelles. For example, a mitochondrion and a nucleus are organelles. Another organelle is a ribosome, which makes protein for the cell to use.

Plant cells have additional organelles such as chloroplasts and cell walls. Chloroplasts convert light energy into food for the plant in a process called photosynthesis. Cell walls are made out of repeating units of glucose molecules (called cellulose) that provide an additional layer of protection for the cell.

How Cells Reproduce

In living things, cells are always changing. Every second, cells are dying and new ones are forming. We will look at two methods of cell division: mitosis and meiosis. Be sure to look over the image of similarities and differences below.

1. Mitosis

Mitosis is the process of cells splitting apart themselves, a one cell decision process. First, the nucleus splits in two. Then, the cell stretches in the middle until it splits, too. Each nucleus becomes the center of a new cell. These cells ,daughter cells, are identical. This process is called mitosis. It reproduces the whole cell, twice, with all the DNA it needs. The DNA is stored in the nucleus, where it is organized into structures called chromosomes. Your skin cells, blood cells, and muscle cells reproduce using mitosis.

2. Meiosis

Meiosis is a kind of cell division that happens in organisms that reproduce with a partner. It makes sex cells, like sperm and egg. One cell divides twice and creates four new cells. Each new cell has only half the number of chromosomes as the original cell. These sex cells are all a little different. When a sperm and egg join, they make a zygote with a full set of chromosomes—half from each parent.

Although mitosis and meiosis follow the same basic steps, they have more differences than similarities. Most of these differences relate back to the fact that, although both are needed for cell replication, mitosis and meiosis have different purposes: mitosis replaces body cells with identical copies, while meiosis creates genetically different sex cells that will be used to create an entirely new organism.

Primary and Secondary Sources

Primary sources are original materials or firsthand information. These come directly from the time or event you are studying. Examples include diaries, letters, photographs, speeches, and interviews. They give you a direct look at what happened.

Secondary sources are secondhand information. They are created by someone who wasn't directly involved in the event. These sources interpret or analyze primary sources. Examples include textbooks, articles, and documentaries. They help explain or summarize what happened.

The Impact on Native Americans

The early history of America is marked by tragedy.

When Europeans arrived, they brought diseases and warfare that devastated Native American populations. At the time of Christopher Columbus's arrival in 1492, millions of Native Americans inhabited North America. However, the introduction of new diseases led to catastrophic mortality rates; in some regions, up to 90 percent of Native Americans perished.

In addition to the impact of disease, many Native Americans lost their lives in conflicts with European settlers. Tens of thousands more were forcibly removed from their ancestral lands, leading to profound disruptions in their communities and cultures.

The First Congress

The colonists' resistance to British rule grew increasingly fervent throughout the 1770s, culminating in significant protests and acts of defiance. In 1774, representatives from various British colonies convened in Philadelphia for the First Continental Congress, marking a pivotal moment in American history. Over several weeks of intense debate and discussion, these leaders strategized about how to respond to the escalating tensions with the British government.

This gathering also ignited discussions among some leaders about the possibility of independence. Their fervent rhetoric inspired their fellow delegates and planted the seeds of a revolutionary spirit. This sentiment would soon pave the way for the colonies to break free from British rule and ultimately declare their independence, shaping the course of history.

The Progressive Era

While segregation ruled in the South, the North faced other issues. The big northern cities grew crowded as people came to find jobs. Poor people lived and worked in horrible conditions. At the same time, the people who owned the factories and railroads grew rich and powerful.

Some Americans fought for better conditions for poor people. They wanted poor people to have the same rights as rich people. This period in America was called the Progressive Era because it was a time when the country made progress. The Progressive Era lasted from about 1890 to 1920.

World War 1

Meanwhile, there were problems in the rest of the world. The great powers of the world were based in Europe, and they were fierce rivals.

In 1914, fighting broke out. It was the beginning of World War 1 (also called the First World War). On one side were Germany, Austria-Hungary, and Italy. The huge Ottoman Empire joined the Central Powers.

The war spread into Africa. Japan joined the Allies. A few countries remained neutral. There were many battles, and hundreds of thousands of soldiers died.

New borders bring new problems

Germany had to give up a lot of territory and had to pay the Allies for damages and losses in their countries.

What are tissues and organs?

Tissue is made of similar cells that group together to do a job in the body. Humans have four types of tissue:

Connective tissue makes bones, blood, tendons, ligaments, and parts of organs.

Respiratory system

The respiratory system is all the organs and tissues that get oxygen to your tissues. Your cells need oxygen to live. When you inhale (breathe air in) your lungs take in oxygen for your body. When you exhale (breathe air out) your lungs get rid of carbon dioxide.

A Pulmonologist is a physician who specializes in the respiratory system.

These are the lungs of a non–smoker and a smoker. Cigarette smoke hurts the lungs. So does living in places with a lot of air pollution.

Cardiovascular system

The cardiovascular system carries oxygen and nutrients around the body. It also removes waste. It uses blood in a system of blood vessels (veins and arteries) to do this. The major organ of the circulatory system is the heart. The heart drives this system. First, it pumps blood to the lungs to pick up oxygen and leaves carbon dioxide behind. Then the heart pumps blood through the body. The blood gives oxygen to cells and picks up carbon dioxide. The blood finally returns to the heart to begin its cycle again. Blood travels through the blood vessels from large arteries, to smaller arteries, to capillaries, to small veins, to large veins, and back to the heart.

A cardiologist is a doctor who specializes in treating diseases of the cardiovascular system — mainly the heart and blood vessels.

How Heredity Works

We learned that genes give us our traits, such as hair color. We also learned that we get our genes from our parents. So our traits come from our parents. This is why children look like their parents. We call this heredity.

But what if a father has black straight hair while the mother has brown curly hair?

If their children all have brown curly hair, the mother’s hair gene is most likely dominant. It was the stronger one. The father’s black hair gene was most likely the recessive gene – it stayed in the background. For a person to inherit black hair in this case, they would have to inherit two recessive alleles or genes for that trait.

Heredity Experiment

In 1865, a scientist named Gregor Mendel used an experiment to show how heredity and dominant genes work.

Mendel used peas for his experiment. Just like people, peas get one copy of DNA from each parent pea! Peas can be green (G) or yellow (Y). A pea with two yellow genes (YY) is yellow, and a pea with two green genes (GG) is green. But what happens if a pea is YG? One of the genes is dominant. The dominant gene controls whether it is green or yellow.

Let’s look at this in a diagram.

This diagram shows the genetics of peas. Allele means the gene version the pea has. Look at how the YG in the second row can produce YYs, YGs, or GGs.

In the diagram above, Y is dominant, so peas with both a Y and G are yellow. If a YY pea breeds with a GG pea, the first generation of their offspring is all YG because they get one copy from each. But in later generations, there are other possibilities. This is because sometimes the G is passed on from a YG pea. That means a pea that looks yellow is passing on its green allele. If that green pairs with another green, then it is a GG and will produce a green pea.

Before the discovery of genetics, people were confused as to how a green pea could come from yellow pea parents!

The Punnett square is a square diagram that is used to predict the genotypes of a particular cross or breeding experiment. It is named after Reginald C. Punnett, who devised the approach in 1905. The diagram is used by biologists to determine the probability of an offspring having a particular genotype.

The processes of meiosis and fertilization can be summarized in Punnett squares to make predictions about the genotypes and phenotypes of offspring.

These predictions are accurate for large samples, but random variation in the genetic makeup of the sperm and egg that unite to form each zygote often results in substantial discrepancies between the Punnett square predictions and the outcomes observed in small samples such as individual families.

Each fertilization event is independent of other fertilization events, so the genetic makeup of each child is independent of the genetic makeup of any siblings.

Punnett squares are a useful model for understanding how meiosis and fertilization result in the various possible genotypes and phenotypes of offspring. Pedigrees are a useful model for analyzing inheritance in individual families.

While Punnett squares are a convenient tool to understand Mendelian genetics, they cannot be used in many situations involving complex genetic inheritance. For example, they are not effective in estimating the distribution of genotypes and phenotypes when there is a linkage between two genes. Sometimes, let’s use flowers, for example, the genes splice or “blend” we get multicolor variations where we normally wouldn’t. Often breeders will mate animals in multiple cycles to achieve the rare or unusual combinations as well. For the test, all punnett squares will work as theoretically expected.

Natural Selection

Evolution happens because some members of a species survive better than others. This could be a plant or bird with traits that help it to survive better than another plant or bird. They are more fit – which in evolution means they have more offspring (babies). If they have more offspring, their traits will be passed on more often. So the species will evolve through natural selection.

Here is an example of natural selection.

In many bird species, females choose males with the most colorful feathers. The males may not be stronger or faster, but their bright colors attract females. So the most colorful males produce more offspring than less colorful males. The colorful males have higher fitness.

As more colorful birds reproduce and share their DNA and their traits, the next generation has more color. The species is slowly changing to favor colorful birds.

Here is another example.

A fast snow leopard can kill more prey. She will eat more than a slow leopard, so she is healthier and will have more babies. Her fast genes will pass to more leopards.

In both examples, the traits that make them more fit will be passed down to more offspring through genes.

Artificial selection

Farmers have learned how to use selection to make better food. They choose the fittest members of species to reproduce.



This is called artificial selection. For example, wheat evolved from much smaller wild grasses. Farmers choose the plants with the largest seeds to replant every year. Over thousands of years, wheat now has much larger seeds than wild grass would.

Mutations and adaptations

Evolution can also happen by accident. Sometimes DNA can get copied incorrectly. This is called a mutation. Mutations are very small. Sometimes many mutations are required to produce a visible change.

An adaptation is a mutation that helps organisms be better suited to their environment. If a mutation makes an organism more fit, then natural selection will take place, and the mutation will stay in the species.

What do Ecologists study?

Scientists that study ecology are called ecologists.

Ecologists study several things:

Diversity is the variety of life found on Earth. To describe the diversity of your local park, you would list the birds, trees, flowers, and insects you see there.

Distribution is all the places a species lives. For example, the pale rose finch lives in Egypt, Jordan, Saudi Arabia, and Israel.

Abundance is how many there are of a species. The abundance of pale rose finches in Jordan is about 10,000.

The environment is everything around us. It can mean the city where you live, the air you breathe, and the rocky surface of our planet, Earth.

Interaction is how living things affect other living things and connect to their environment.

Organizing the world

In ecology, scientists organize the world into big regions and smaller areas or groups within them.

The biosphere includes everything on Earth and the atmosphere that surrounds it.

Biomes are regions of the world with similar climates, animals, and plants – for example, a dessert

Ecosystems are sets of living and nonliving things that function together – for example, the trees, animals, and rivers in a rainforest

A community is all of the organisms (living things) in an area – for example, all the fish, birds, insects, and plants that live around a small pond.

A population is a separate group of organisms in one species – for example, the deer that live on one side of a mountain range that never mingles with the deer population on the other side.

Organisms and their habitats

The place where an organism lives is called its habitat.

A habitat provides the things an organism needs to survive. Organisms need the right climate and amount of water. They also need other organisms.

No living thing can live without other living things. All organisms interact with others to survive. Let’s look at some interactions in the natural world:

Resources are the things all organisms need to survive. They include water and maybe shelter. But resources also include other organisms, such as members of their own species to mate with. Resources are also the species of animals and plants they eat.

Many interactions happen when organisms are looking for food. The organisms that hunt and eat other organisms are called predators. The organisms that are being hunted are called prey.

In a competition, something wins and something loses. For example, if a wolf and a bear are both trying to eat a dead deer, they are competing for food.

Organisms also interact through cooperation. For example, “cleaner fish” are small fish that clean parasites off larger fish. The large fish could easily eat the cleaner fish, but they do not. The fish are working together, or cooperating – the cleaner fish get food when they eat the parasites; the larger fish get rid of the parasites. This is a win–win interaction!

Ecosystems tend to remain stable if left undisturbed by humans. Every part of an ecosystem plays a role in keeping it functioning well. However, some species are especially crucial. These species are called keystone species.

Earth’s crust is broken up into tectonic plates. These plates are separate pieces that fit together to form Earth’s surface layer. These plates are huge, bigger than continents. The plates float on the magma below, moving gradually over thousands or millions of years.

This map shows Earth’s tectonic plates. You can see the United States is on the North American Plate.

Types of Tectonic Plate Boundaries

Tectonic plates have three main types of boundaries (edges) that reflect the different ways they move. The three types are:

Divergent boundaries: where plates are moving away from each other  

Convergent boundaries: where plates move towards each other and meet 

Transform boundaries: where plates slide along beside each other

Fossils

Another important part of geology is the study of fossils. Fossils have taught scientists much of what they know about life before humans. Fossils are pieces or imprints (like a footprint) of animals, plants, or other organisms that have been preserved from a long time ago. People often find fossils that are millions of years old.

An AI generated image of a dinosaur fossil

The fossils show where and when different plants and animals lived. Many of the species are extinct, and no longer exist. Some examples of fossils are dinosaur bones and imprints of the shells of sea creatures from millions of years ago and can be found most often in sedimentary rocks.

Minerals

Geologists study minerals, too. A mineral is a nonliving substance that occurs naturally on Earth.

Minerals are everywhere, including in food! Salt, for example, is a mineral. Minerals combine to make other substances. We’ll learn more about minerals later in science class.

The Earth's Atmosphere: A Vital Shield

The atmosphere is the layer of gases that surrounds our planet, Earth. It plays a crucial role in supporting life, protecting us from harmful space elements, and regulating temperatures. Without the atmosphere, life as we know it would not exist.

Composition of the Atmosphere

The atmosphere is made up of a mixture of gases, which include:

78% Nitrogen (N₂): The most abundant gas in the atmosphere. While nitrogen itself is not involved in the breathing process, it helps maintain the balance of gases in the air.

21% Oxygen (O₂): Essential for respiration in humans and animals, oxygen allows us to breathe and provides the fuel our cells need to produce energy.

0.93% Argon (Ar): An inert gas, meaning it doesn't react with other substances easily. Though present in small amounts, argon plays a role in filling the gaps between other gases.

0.039% Carbon Dioxide (CO₂): Though present in small quantities, carbon dioxide is vital for plant life, which absorbs it during photosynthesis. Additionally, CO₂ helps regulate Earth's temperature by trapping some of the Sun's heat.

0.4–1% Water Vapor (H₂O): The amount of water vapor varies depending on weather conditions. Water vapor is important for the water cycle and helps form clouds, rain, and snow.

Trace amounts of other gases: This includes methane (CH₄), nitrous oxide (N₂O), ozone (O₃), neon (Ne), helium (He), krypton (Kr), and hydrogen (H₂). Though these gases make up less than 1% of the atmosphere, some, like ozone, have critical functions.

Layers of the Atmosphere

The Earth's atmosphere is divided into five distinct layers, each with unique characteristics:

Troposphere: This is the lowest layer, where we live and where weather occurs. The troposphere extends up to about 8 to 15 kilometers (5 to 9 miles) above sea level.

Stratosphere: Above the troposphere is the stratosphere, where the ozone layer is found. The ozone layer absorbs most of the Sun's harmful ultraviolet (UV) rays, protecting life on Earth.

Mesosphere: This is the middle layer, where meteors burn up as they enter Earth's atmosphere.

Thermosphere: The thermosphere is characterized by high temperatures and contains the ionosphere, which is important for radio communications and auroras (like the Northern Lights).

Exosphere: The outermost layer, where the atmosphere gradually fades into space. This is where satellites orbit the Earth.

Importance of the Atmosphere

Breathing and Life Support: The atmosphere provides the oxygen we breathe and helps regulate the gases needed for various biological processes. Without it, life could not survive.

Temperature Regulation: The atmosphere acts as a blanket, trapping heat from the Sun and preventing the Earth from becoming too cold at night. This process is known as the greenhouse effect.

Protection from UV Radiation: The ozone layer within the stratosphere absorbs most of the harmful ultraviolet (UV) radiation from the Sun, protecting humans and animals from skin cancer and other damage caused by UV rays.

Weather and Climate: The atmosphere is responsible for the Earth's weather and climate systems. It drives wind patterns, precipitation, and the formation of storms.

What is Air Pressure

The atmosphere is made of molecules or tiny particles of substances. Molecules are too small to see, but they have weight and movement. The molecules’ weight and movement cause atmospheric pressure – what people call air pressure.

When you press a hand pump, it pushes the air within the tube, which moves and creates pressure that makes the needle move. When you release the pump, the pressure of air on the needle decreases and it moves back

Pressure is the weight of something pushing down. Where air molecules are close together, the air is heavier, and air pressure is high. Air molecules are more spread out higher up in the atmosphere. There, the air is lighter, and air pressure goes down.

Weather and Meteorology

Changes in pressure affect the world’s weather. As you know, the weather changes all the time. Changes are caused by changing conditions in the lower atmosphere. Weather conditions include rain and snow, wind, low and high pressure, storminess, cloudiness, and fluctuations in temperature.

Weather is happening outside daily. This forecast shows the weather for a week.

Meteorology is a branch of Earth science focused on the study of the atmosphere and weather patterns. Scientists who specialize in predicting and studying the weather are known as meteorologists. These experts monitor and analyze the movement of weather systems across the globe using advanced tools such as satellite imagery, radar, and computer models. Meteorologists can provide forecasts that predict whether it will be sunny, rainy, or stormy today, tomorrow, or even several days in advance.

The climate of an area is determined by the average weather patterns taken over a long period of time. Understanding a region’s climate helps us to know what type of weather we can expect.

Positive and Negative Charges

Protons and neutrons are attracted (pulled together) by a force called an electrical charge. A charge is the amount of electricity in an object. For your test, you will need to know:

Electrons are particles with a negative (minus or –) charge of 1.

Protons are particles with a positive (plus or +) charge of 1. 

Neutrons are particles with no charge: they are neutral.

Ions are groups of atoms with a positive or negative charge.

The opposite charges are attracted to each other, and this force is one of the things that hold the atom together.

Atoms start with the same number of electrons and protons, so they have zero electrical charge. But sometimes they lose or gain electrons. When the number of electrons is not the same as the number of protons, the charge is no longer zero, and the atom becomes charged. A charged atom (one with an electric charge) is called an ion.

If there are more electrons, the atom is negatively charged. It becomes an anion. But if there are more protons, then the atom is positively charged. This type of atom is a cation.

Elements

Scientists have discovered and named only 118 different types of atoms. When atoms of one kind group together, they make elements. Elements are matter with only one kind of atom and are substances that cannot be broken. Hydrogen and oxygen are elements. So are gold and carbon. Each element has a chemical symbol. Hydrogen, H, and Gold, Au; for example.

Each element’s atom has a different number of protons from any other element’s atom. That number is anywhere from 1 to 118 because there are 118 known types of atoms. As we saw in the diagram of a calcium atom above, the number of protons (20) is what makes that atom a calcium atom. It’s the same for all the other atoms in elements, from gold (79 protons) to oxygen (8 protons). The number of protons tells the element what to be and tells us what it is!

What is a substance in science?

You hear people talking about substances in everyday English. For example, they may say glue is a sticky substance. But in science, substance means matter made of specific atoms. Elements are one kind of substance. If two different elements join together, you get a new substance in definite proportions, this is called a compound. Water is a great example of a compound. At room temperature water is a liquid, however separately, Hydrogen and Oxygen are gasses. A mixture is a physical combination of two or more substances that keep their own properties; like saltwater.

Molecules and compounds

This is a diagram of a water molecule. Water molecules have one oxygen (O) atom and two hydrogen (H) atoms.

We learned that every atom is a tiny piece of matter. We learned that atoms of one kind group together to form elements. When atoms join together, they form a molecule. A molecule with two or more atoms is held together with bonds. Oxygen, for example, is made of oxygen molecules, which are oxygen atoms bonded together.

When atoms or molecules of different elements join together, they become a compound. All substances on the earth except elements are compounds. For example, one atom of oxygen combined with two atoms of hydrogen makes the compound we call water.

How do molecules join together?

To join together, molecules make chemical bonds. Chemical bonds are like glue. They join atoms together to form molecules and they join molecules together to make compounds.

There are two main kinds of chemical bonds you will need to recognize. Ionic bonds form when a metal joins with a nonmetal. Ionic bonds will gain or lose electrons to become ions that attract ions to hold the compound together; like table salt. Covalent bonds form when two non–metallic ions join together. In covalent bonds, atoms will share a pair of electrons; one electron each; like water. In the next lesson, we’ll learn what makes substances join and change.

Physical Changes

In science, we learn that molecules (the building blocks of matter) can bond together to form various substances. These substances can change their form or "state" based on temperature and other conditions.

The four main states of matter you'll need to know for your GED test are solids, liquids, gases, and plasma.

Solids: In solids, the molecules are packed tightly together, which is why solids keep their shape. For example, a rock, wood, or a desk stays the same shape unless you forcefully break it.

Liquids: In liquids, the molecules are more loosely packed, allowing them to flow and take the shape of their container. Think about water, juice, or oil—they can be poured, and they’ll fill up the bottom of whatever container they’re in.

Gases: In gases, the molecules are spread out and move freely. This is why gases don’t have a definite shape and can expand to fill any space. For instance, the air we breathe is made up of gases like oxygen and nitrogen. If you blow up a balloon, the gas inside takes up all the space within the balloon.

Plasma: Plasma is the fourth state of matter and is different from the other three. In plasma, the gas molecules become electrically charged, or "ionized," because they have so much energy. Plasma is found in very hot places, like the sun and stars, where temperatures are extremely high. You can also find plasma in things like lightning and neon signs. Plasma doesn’t have a fixed shape, like gases, but it behaves differently because of the charged particles.

These changes in state happen when temperature and pressure conditions change. For example, heating a solid like ice turns it into liquid water, and heating water turns it into steam (gas). If you heat gas to extremely high temperatures, it becomes plasma.

Ice melts to make water and water evaporates to form gases. This is one example of a matter changing its state

The pictures above show water in all three states of matter, or phases: ice (solid), water (liquid), and steam (gas). Both gasses and liquids are fluids, which means they can change shape according to where they are.

Gasses are like air. In a gas, there is a lot of space between the molecules. Gasses have a lot of volume when they are not contained because they can spread out. They have no definite shape and no definite volume.

Liquids are like milk. They have less space between molecules. They can change shape. They have definite volume but not shape.

Solids are like wood. They have very little space in between the molecules. They have a more fixed shape. They have definite shape and volume.

Substances can melt from solid to liquid and they can boil from liquid to gas. The changes can go in the other direction too. 

Condensing is what happens when a gas changes to a liquid. For example, water vapor turning into rain drops.

Freezing is a process that changes a liquid into a solid, like freezing water to become ice. 

In these physical changes, atoms and molecules move from one state to another. But the substance stayed the same. Water is still water when it is ice or steam. That’s because it still has the same molecules made of hydrogen and oxygen.

Reactants and Products

In a chemical reaction, we start with reactants—these are the substances that take part in the reaction. After the reaction happens, we get products, which are the new substances that are created.

Even though the products look different from the reactants, the atoms don’t disappear or get destroyed. Instead, they get rearranged to form new substances. This is a key idea in chemistry: matter is never lost or created in a chemical reaction; it just changes form.

This diagram shows what happens to atoms or molecules during four different types of chemical reactions. The reactants are to the left of the arrows. The products are to the right of the arrows. Note that none of the atoms went away in the reaction. They just rearranged themselves.

Many chemical reactions fall into one of the types of reaction in the diagram above. Let’s apply the reactions to processes in real life:

Synthesis or combination: Two substances combine to make one new substance.

Example: A simple everyday example is rust forming on a nail. When iron (Fe) in the nail combines with oxygen (O₂) in the air, they form a new substance called iron oxide (Fe₂O₃), or rust.

Decomposition: One substance turns into two new ones.

Example: Heating up sugar is a decomposition reaction. When you heat sugar in a pan, it breaks down into carbon (which turns it black) and water vapor, causing it to caramelize or burn.

Single Displacement (exchange): A molecule or atom from one substance switches to another substance.

Example: Cleaning tarnished silver with aluminum foil and baking soda is a single displacement reaction. The aluminum (Al) takes the tarnish (sulfur) off the silver (Ag), making it shiny again.

Double Displacement (exchange): One molecule or atom from each substance switches to the other substance.

Example: Mixing baking soda with vinegar is a classic example of double displacement. When you mix baking soda and vinegar, they swap parts and form carbon dioxide (CO₂), which creates bubbles.

Combustion and Catalysts

Another important reaction is combustion.

Combustion is what happens when wood burns. A reactant, usually one that contains carbon, combines with oxygen, usually from the air, and releases energy. A catalyst is anything that helps a chemical reaction occur or speeds it up. Adding gasoline to a fire is a catalyst because it makes the fire burst into flames.

Burning of wood is combustion

Let's look at an example:

When wood burns in a combustion process, the reactants and catalysts involved are as follows:

Reactants

Wood: The primary reactant, which is composed mainly of cellulose (C6H10O5), hemicellulose, and lignin.

Oxygen (O2): From the air, it reacts with the components of wood to produce heat, light, carbon dioxide, and water.

Catalysts

In the combustion process of wood, catalysts are not typically used. However, certain conditions or substances can facilitate the combustion process:

Heat: While not a catalyst in the traditional sense, heat is necessary to initiate the combustion process by breaking down the wood's complex molecules into simpler forms that can react with oxygen.

Charcoal or Ash: In some cases, residual charcoal or ash from previous burns can act to maintain and spread the heat within the fire, although they do not serve as true catalysts.

In most cases, combustion needs an external heat source to start the reaction.

Exothermic and Endothermic Reaction

Energy is involved in all chemical reactions. A reaction in which the reactants absorb energy from their surroundings is an endothermic reaction. For example, a scrambled egg solidifies when you add heat (energy) to it.

A reaction in which energy is given off with the products, usually in the form of heat or light, is an exothermic reaction. When you burn wood in a fireplace, for example, heat and light energy or giving off activation energy is the amount of energy needed to get the reactions going.
